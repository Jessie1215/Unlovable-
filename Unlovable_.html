Written by Prince Laplana

Unlovable?

My Life So Far

Submitted by
Case, Grade 12
Subject
English (Personal Narrative)
Prepared for
Ma’am Reyes

Ma’am said “write a narrative report about your life.” She did not say “keep it short,” so I’m taking that as permission.

First, my name. It’s Case. Yes, it sounds like a girl’s name. No, I’m not one. I’ve had this conversation since Grade 1 and I’m tired of it, so let’s move on.

Second, I’m a senior. People think seniors are the best of the best, the big egos, the ones who rule the hallway. I’m not. I’m a regular guy who sits near the back, eats the same lunch every day, and is quietly, permanently NGSB. No Girlfriend Since Birth. It’s practically on my ID.

Third, I know love stories. I know them very well. I’ve watched enough K-dramas and anime to teach a semester on it. The rain scene, the wrist grab, the “I’ll wait for you” speech at the train station. I even keep a private ranking of tsunderes, which is the word for a character who acts cold and prickly on the outside and is soft on the inside. I know how it’s supposed to go. The only thing I don’t know is how it feels.

That’s what this report is about. Ma’am asked for my life, and I’m giving her the forty days where it got interesting. In those forty days I met two girls who could have walked straight out of an anime. I want it on record that I did not ask for this.

Day−14The Announcement

It was a normal Monday. The fan was broken, the chalk smelled like old rain, and I was drawing a tiny sword on my notebook when Ma’am Reyes tapped the board.

“Two weeks from now, JSTP begins.”

JSTP. The Joint School Training Program. Forty days where our school pairs up with a school across the city, and seniors from both sides train together. Leadership workshops, community projects, a schedule printed on three pages. Half the class groaned. The other half pretended to groan but was secretly excited, because forty days with new people is forty days of new possibilities.

I didn’t groan. I looked at the back-left corner of the room instead.

That’s where Kale sits.

Yes, Kale. Like the vegetable. Bitter, expensive, and constantly telling you she’s good for you.

She has platinum hair that she swears is natural, grey eyes that make you feel graded, and skin so pale she looks like she was built from the good porcelain. Her uniform is tailored so sharply you could slice bread with the pleats. Her dad sits on the school board, and she mentions it about once a week, in the tone of someone mentioning the weather. Mico calls her “Malfoid” behind her back, because she looks like what you’d get if a certain famous blond wizard rival had been born a girl and transferred to our school. Nobody has ever said it to her face and lived.

She speaks to me in exactly two modes: insult and silence. I’m “Sword Boy.” My lunch is “a cry for help.” Once, in Grade 11, she looked at my notebook doodle for a long time and said, “It is… not completely terrible.” Then she turned around so fast her ponytail snapped, and the tips of her ears went red.

I have been thinking about that sentence for seven months. I have it memorized. I’m aware that this is a problem.

She also has a boyfriend.

His name is Dion. He’s in the other Grade 12 section, he’s tall, he plays basketball, and he’s nice. That’s the worst part. If he were a jerk, I could have a whole villain arc. But he’s the only person alive who can say “Kale, stop” and have her actually stop. And once, he lent me his umbrella and didn’t ask for it back. You can’t hate a guy like that. You can only quietly lose to him.

So that’s my situation. A crush on a girl who’s taken, who calls me names, who I somehow find incredibly cute when she does it. Forty days of training ahead. A heart that has only ever been used for watching other people’s love stories.

Wahaha. We good.

Day0The Opening Ceremony

The other school’s students arrived in two white buses. They filed into our gym wearing maroon uniforms, and for one strange minute the whole place looked like two different colors of ocean trying to share a bathtub.

Their school was Aurelian Academy, famous for its culinary program. Half the restaurants in the city are run by Aurelian graduates, and every one of them will tell you so within five minutes of meeting you.

We were sent to our assigned groups. Mine was Group 6, eight people, four from each school. I sat alone at the end of a bench, as usual, doodling on my knee. The loneliness hit a little. I won’t lie.

Then a shadow fell across me.

She was standing there with perfect posture, like she’d been balancing a book on her head since birth. Long hair tied back with a ribbon that cost more than my shoes. A uniform with no wrinkles anywhere, which should be impossible after a bus ride. She looked at the empty space beside me. She looked at me. She took a handkerchief out of her sleeve and wiped the bench.

“I assume this is the only seat left,” she said. “Regrettably.”

She sat. Her eyes dropped to my knee.

“That sword,” she said, “has no sense of proportion.”

“It’s losing.”

“It deserves to.”

I had a strange feeling that I’d been insulted by someone who had thought about it carefully first. That’s different from Kale. Kale insults you like a thrown shoe. This girl did it like a surgeon.

“Kris,” she said, and did not offer her hand. “Aurelian Academy. You’ve heard of it, of course.”

“No.”

The silence that followed was so offended I could feel it on my skin.

“Case,” I said quickly.

“Hm. A name with no confidence in it.”

“It’s a boy’s name.”

“If you say so.”

I’m putting this in my report because Ma’am said to include important moments, and I didn’t know it was one yet. I just knew I’d met a girl who could out-sentence Kale, and that she had wiped a bench for me, even if she’d done it to protect herself.

Day3Teamwork Is a Scam

Our first group task was building a bridge out of newspaper and tape that could hold a coconut. Nobody explained why.

Group 6 was a disaster. Mico argued with everybody. Jana kept saying “let’s be strategic” without ever saying a strategy. The two quiet guys from Aurelian stared at the newspaper as if it owed them money.

Kris watched this for exactly ninety seconds. Then she stood up.

“Stop. All of you. You are folding it wrong.”

She took over like a general. She rolled a sheet into a tight tube, tested the tape between her fingers, and sniffed it. “Cheap adhesive. Tragic.” She ordered Jana to hold, Mico to be quiet, and me to roll. When I rolled badly, she took the paper from my hands, fixed it without comment, and gave it back. When I rolled correctly, she said nothing. I decided that silence was a compliment. I was right, but it took me weeks to be sure.

Our bridge held the coconut for ten full seconds. Every other group’s lasted less than three. The prize was a pack of crackers.

Kris took one, bit it, and closed her eyes.

“Stale,” she said. “Wheat flour from a budget mill. Too much salt, applied carelessly. And it was stored beside laundry detergent. You can taste the lavender.”

Group 6 stared. I checked the crate. The detergent beside it said Lavender Fresh.

“Is that so impressive?” she said.

“God Tongue,” Mico whispered. I know that reference. I nearly fell off my chair. Kris pretended she hadn’t heard, but her ear turned pink.

She finished her half of the crackers anyway, every crumb. Then she pushed the other half across the table toward me.

“Take it. I don’t intend to finish it.”

She had already finished hers. I looked at the half she’d pushed over and understood that she’d saved it for me from the beginning.

I told myself it meant nothing. K-drama rule number one: the main character never notices the real love interest until episode twelve.

Rule number two: it’s usually the one who shared the crackers.

I told myself to stop watching so many dramas.

Day6The Boyfriend Problem

On the sixth day, Kale ambushed me at the canteen.

“Sword Boy. Sit.”

It wasn’t an invitation. It was a verdict. I sat. Dion was already there, grinning at me like a golden retriever, and Kale was stirring her drink with the cold precision of someone who had decided today would be tolerable.

“Don’t misunderstand,” she said, not looking at me. “The only reason you’re here is because Dion insisted.”

“I didn’t insist,” said Dion. “You said, ‘go get him.’”

“I said no such thing.”

“You said it twice.”

She kicked him under the table. He laughed. I watched her ears go red, the same red as in Grade 11, and my stupid heart did its stupid thing.

She looked at my tray. “That rice has the structural integrity of regret.” She stole three of my fries anyway, one at a time, as if each one were a punishment. “These are cold. Appalling.”

“You can stop eating them.”

“I will decide when I stop.”

Dion mouthed, She likes you, and I mouthed, She hates me, and he shrugged like both could be true. They could. I’ve learned that, with Kale, they usually are.

Then she paused. Her grey eyes had drifted over my shoulder to the far side of the canteen.

“Who is that?”

I turned. Kris was at a table of maroon uniforms, back as straight as a ruler. She was looking right at me. She raised one eyebrow, slowly, as if to say, Really, Case? Fries?

“That’s Kris,” I said. “Group 6. From Aurelian.”

“She’s looking at you like you’re a dish that got sent back to the kitchen.” Kale narrowed her eyes. “You made a friend from the other school already. Hmph. Fast.”

“Are you… jealous?”

“Of what?” Her voice went sharp enough to cut glass. “Don’t flatter yourself.” She stood, flipped her hair, and walked off so fast she forgot her drink.

Dion looked at the drink. He looked at me.

“Bro,” he said gently. “She forgot her drink.”

I’ve never seen Kale forget anything in her life.

Day9The Rain

I’ll be honest. I had been waiting my whole life for a rain scene.

I’d planned for it. In every drama, there’s an afternoon when the sky opens up and the lead has no umbrella, and someone appears. I knew exactly how it should go.

On Day 9, it rained. A real downpour, the kind that turns the school yard into a lake. And I had no umbrella.

Unfortunately, neither did anyone else, except one person.

Kris stood under the covered walkway with a large black umbrella, perfectly dry, perfectly poised, watching the rain like it had arrived late to a reservation.

“It’s a one-person umbrella,” she said before I could open my mouth. “It was not designed for charity.”

“I didn’t ask.”

“You were thinking it loudly.”

A pause. The rain hammered the roof. She sighed deeply, as if the weight of the world’s inconveniences rested on her shoulders. Then she stepped beside me and tilted the umbrella until it covered both of us.

“Don’t misunderstand,” she said, staring forward. “Dampness is a disservice to my uniform. Your proximity is incidental.”

“Of course.”

“Entirely incidental.”

We walked to the gate that way, an inch of rain between my left shoulder and the edge of the umbrella. She’d tilted it a little more toward me than herself. I noticed, because her right sleeve was getting soaked. I didn’t say anything. I wasn’t brave enough to ruin it.

Somewhere between the walkway and the gate, I said, “You watch K-dramas, don’t you?”

“Certainly not.”

“You know what a rain scene is.”

“I have encountered them. Incidentally.” She sniffed. “The second lead should never win. It’s a structural flaw in the genre.”

I stopped walking. “You do watch them.”

“I said I have encountered them.”

I laughed. She turned her face away, but not fast enough to hide her mouth twitching.

At the gate, she handed me a folded handkerchief for my wet sleeve. It was monogrammed. K.N. I didn’t ask what the N stood for.

“I’m usually here at this time,” she said. “If you happened to be here also, it would be… tolerable.”

And she walked off through the drizzle with her back straight and her sleeve dripping, before I could answer.

Day12A Note to Myself

(Ma’am, this part isn’t really for the report. But it’s true, so I’m leaving it in.)

I’ve been noticing something strange. When something funny happens, my first thought used to be, “I wish Kale saw that.” Lately, it’s, “I need to tell Kris.”

I don’t like it. I don’t like it at all. I have a crush. I’ve had it for seven months. It has a history. It has a favorite insult. You can’t just replace a seven-month crush with a person you met twelve days ago, who doesn’t even go to your school, who looks at my cooking with open contempt and my drawings like they’re evidence in a trial.

That’s not how this works. That’s not how any of this works.

Anyway. I told Kris about the cat that sleeps on our school gate, and she said, “Unacceptable. Cats belong indoors.” Then she asked what color it was. Then she asked what it ate. Then she asked if it had a name.

“Biscuit,” I said.

“A reasonable name,” she said, like she was approving a passport.

I felt it again. The small warm thing in my chest, the one with no name.

I’m calling it indigestion until further notice.

Day14The Tasting

The coordinators scheduled a cooking day. Each group had to make a dish for the school’s feeding program. Group 6 drew rice, eggs, and garlic.

I said I could make garlic fried rice. I make it every morning. I had never been so confident and so wrong.

Kris stood next to me, arms folded, watching me work the way a bomb technician watches a wire.

“The heat is too high,” she said.

“It’s fine.”

“It is not fine. The garlic is going to burn.”

“It’s fine.”

The garlic burned. I served her a plate anyway, because I’m a man of dignity.

She took a spoonful and closed her eyes. The whole group went quiet. I swear even the rice held its breath.

“Burnt garlic,” she said. “Bitter. The rice is too wet, which means you didn’t let it cool before frying. The egg is… acceptable.”

“Acceptable is good,” I said, desperately.

“Acceptable is acceptable.” She opened her eyes and looked at me. “Do you have anything to say in your defense?”

“I made it at five a.m. and I was distracted.”

“By what?”

I could not say “by you.” I said, “By the sword.”

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she took another spoonful. And another. And another.

When the plate was empty, she set down the spoon carefully, wiped her mouth with her handkerchief, and said, “Wasting food is a crime. Don’t misunderstand.”

I nodded. Then, very quietly, as if the words had slipped out without her permission, she said, “No one has cooked for me in a long time who wasn’t trying to impress me.”

I didn’t know what to say. She didn’t look at me. She pretended to adjust her sleeve.

“It was bad,” she added quickly. “Objectively. Do not repeat that story.”

I’ve never told anyone. Until now, in a report that Ma’am Reyes is going to read. Hi, Ma’am.

Day17Dion

I wasn’t expecting Dion to find me alone behind the gym.

He looked tired. Not basketball-tired. The other kind.

“Hey, Case. Got a minute?”

I had several. “Sure.”

“You’re friends with Kale, right? Like, actually friends?”

My stomach dropped about three floors. “I’m… the person she insults.”

“Yeah. That’s what I mean.” He rubbed the back of his neck. “She’s been quiet lately. Even quieter. She says she’s fine. She says it like she’s reading a legal statement. You know how she is. I gave her a keychain for our anniversary and she called it ‘a cheap trinket’ for two days. Then I saw it on her bag. Hanging right there.”

“That sounds like Kale.”

“I don’t know if it’s me. I don’t know if it’s the JSTP. I just… I can’t tell what she means anymore.”

I could have said anything. I could have said something that made me look good. I could have said, “Maybe she needs someone who understands her,” and made myself the hero of a story I’m very much not the hero of.

But Dion had lent me an umbrella once. So I said, “Have you asked her?”

He blinked. “No.”

“Maybe start there.”

He nodded slowly, like I’d said something profound. I hadn’t. It was the most basic advice in the world. But he thanked me anyway, and I stood there behind the gym feeling like the most useless, most honest guy alive.

That night, I told Kris about it over the phone, because by then we had exchanged numbers for “group project reasons” that were completely made up.

“That was a decent thing to do,” she said.

“It was the worst thing I’ve ever done. I gave my crush’s boyfriend relationship advice.”

“Yes.” Her voice went quiet. “That is what makes it decent. Don’t let it go to your head.”

I didn’t have a reply. I stared at my ceiling for a very long time.

Day21The Halfway Point

Twenty-one days. Half the program. The coordinators made us write letters to ourselves, to be opened on the last day.

I wrote, “Dear Case, I hope you were brave.” I looked at it for a long time. Then I added, “At least a little.”

Kris wrote hers on the other side of the table, curled around the paper like a spy. Her handwriting was so perfect it looked printed.

“What did you write?” I asked.

“That is none of your business.”

“It’s my business if it’s about the sword.”

“It is not about the sword.” She folded it into a perfect square, edges aligned to the millimeter. “Not primarily.”

That afternoon we had a free period, and Kale found me on the bleachers. She threw a bottle of juice at my chest without warning. I caught it by luck.

“Don’t read into it,” she said. “I bought two by mistake.”

She sat down two feet away, which for Kale is basically leaning on my shoulder. Her eyes were red. Her chin was up. She looked like a queen at a funeral who’d decided no one would know.

“Are you okay?”

“It’s hay fever.”

“It’s October.”

“It is a late hay fever.” She glared at the field. After a long silence she said, very fast, “I’m going to break up with Dion. Don’t look at me like that. It is not because of you, Sword Boy. Do not be delusional.”

My heart did the cruel, hopeful thing. This is it. This is the drama scene.

She didn’t turn. She kept glaring at the field.

“He never complains,” she said. “I’ve been awful to him for a year. I call his gifts trinkets. I say things I don’t mean and he just smiles. And I can’t tell if I love him or if I’m just used to him being kind to me.” Her voice cracked on kind. She cleared her throat like it had personally offended her. “That’s a fault in him, obviously.”

I sat very still. There’s a version of me, the one that lives in the dramas, who would have said something right then. Who would’ve said, I’ve liked you for seven months. Who would have made it about himself.

But when I looked at her face, what I felt wasn’t hope. It was sadness for her, and a strange, small relief that I wasn’t the thing she needed to fix.

“Be kind to him,” I said. “Even if you break up. He’s a good guy.”

“I’m always kind.”

“You called a keychain a cheap trinket.”

Her whole face went pink. “He told you that?!”

“He said you hung it on your bag.”

“That is a coincidence.” She stood up so fast her hair swung. “Thank you. For the… listening. It was tolerable.” She walked three steps, stopped, and said without turning around, “Don’t make it weird.”

“I never make anything weird.”

“You are constantly weird.” And she marched off, spine straight, ears glowing red like traffic lights.

I sat there with two juices and a feeling I couldn’t name. It wasn’t heartbreak. It was more like watching a photograph of a place I used to love. I was fond of it. I remembered it. But I wasn’t sure I lived there anymore.

Day26The Crack in the Plan

I’ve been avoiding something in this report, so I’ll say it plainly.

I don’t think I like Kale the way I used to.

Not because there’s anything wrong with her. She’s still sharp and funny and secretly kinder than she wants anyone to know. She still insults my lunch. But when she does it now, I laugh instead of aching. I like her the way you like a good rivalry, the way you like a storm you’ve gotten used to.

And I don’t know when I moved out of the crush.

What scares me is that I think I know who moved in.

I sat in the canteen on Day 26 and watched Kris across the room. She was sitting with her fellow Aurelian students, back straight, napkin folded on her lap, listening to someone talk with the expression of a judge on a cooking show. Then she looked over and caught my eye.

For exactly half a second, with total dignity, she stuck out her tongue at me.

Then she turned around, horrified at herself, and took a long sip of her tea.

I laughed so hard I had to put down my tray. And I wasn’t doing it for a drama. There was no camera. There was no soundtrack. It was just two people being ridiculous at each other across a room, even though one of us would rather die than admit it.

I think that was the first real love-story moment of my life. And it didn’t look anything like the ones on screen. There was no rain. No wrist grab. Nobody delivered a speech.

It was just a tongue. And it was enough.

Day29Mico Ruins Everything (As Usual)

Group 6 had to present a community project to the whole assembly. Ours was a recycling drive. Mico was supposed to give the closing statement, but he came down with a mysterious illness that appeared to be “didn’t write anything.”

So the closing fell to me.

I don’t do public speaking. I do back-of-the-room sketching and quiet commentary. But there I was, standing on the stage with eight hundred eyes on me, and my knees were doing their own separate presentation.

I found Kris in the crowd. She sat with her hands folded and her chin lifted, and she made no expression at all. Then she raised two fingers, very slightly, in the shape of a tiny sword. Don’t lose it.

I took a breath.

“Recycling isn’t about the planet,” I said. “I mean, it is. But mostly it’s about believing that something you think is finished can still be useful. That an old thing can become a new thing, if somebody’s patient enough.”

I wasn’t really talking about plastic bottles anymore, and I think she knew it.

The applause was bigger than we deserved. When I got offstage, my hands were shaking, and Kris was already waiting by the curtain with her arms crossed.

“Well?” I said.

“Acceptable.”

I knew by now that this was the highest score she had ever given anyone in my presence. I tried not to smile and failed completely.

“Do not look so pleased,” she said. “The pacing in the second half was uneven.”

“Okay.”

“And you rushed the ending.”

“Okay.”

“But the part about the old thing becoming new…” She looked away, at a spot on the wall that suddenly seemed very interesting. “That was not… unacceptable.”

Then she bumped her shoulder against mine, very quickly, and left it there a second longer than she needed to.

I am a certified NGSB. I have no experience with this. But I know what a second longer means.

Day33The Almost

The coordinators scheduled a night camp. Tents on the school field, bonfire, stars, the whole cliché. Obviously, I was thrilled and terrified.

Somewhere around ten p.m., while everybody else played a loud game involving a ukulele and bad singing, Kris tapped my arm and tilted her head toward the dark end of the field.

We sat on the grass, away from the fire. The sky was ridiculous with stars. She’d brought a blanket and spread it out with military precision, and then sat on the very edge of it, like she was trying to be fair about the space.

For a while neither of us said anything.

“Case,” she said finally. “Do you truly believe you are unlovable?”

I turned to look at her. “Where did you hear that?”

“You said it on Day 9, under my umbrella. ‘People like me don’t get the rain scene.’ You laughed, but it was not a real laugh.” She stared at the stars. “I don’t tolerate inaccurate claims.”

I had no memory of saying it. Which meant I’d said it the way you say things you’ve believed for a long time, without noticing.

“I don’t know,” I said honestly. “I’ve never really tested it.”

“Hm.” She was quiet. Then, so low I almost missed it: “I have the opposite problem.”

“What do you mean?”

“My grandmother started a restaurant. Narvaez. K.N. is Kristina Narvaez, since you’ve been too polite to ask.” Her fingers tightened on her sleeve. “Everyone expects the Narvaez tongue to be flawless. I have not been allowed to cook anything that was not perfect since I was nine years old. I am admired. I am feared. I am extremely good at being impressive.” She paused. “But I don’t know whether anyone would stay if I were only… ordinary. Burnt garlic. Wet rice.”

My chest hurt in an unfamiliar way. “On Day 14 you ate a whole plate of my burnt garlic rice,” I said. “Every last spoonful.”

“That was an exception.”

“I know.” I smiled at the grass. “That’s why I noticed.”

Her hand was on the blanket between us. Mine was two inches away. Two inches. I’ve seen this scene in sixty dramas. This is where the music swells. This is where the lead closes the gap.

But it was Kris who moved first. Her little finger slid half an inch across the cloth. Half an inch. It was the bravest thing I’ve ever seen anyone do with a pinky.

I looked at her hand. I looked at mine.

And then Mico screamed from across the field that somebody had set a marshmallow on fire, and the moment shattered into a hundred pieces, and Kris pulled her hand back like it had been burned and stood up and brushed off her skirt with total composure.

“We should assist,” she said. Her voice was a half-tone too high.

“Yeah,” I said. “We should.”

I’ve decided to blame Mico for the rest of my life.

Day36What Kale Said

Kale broke up with Dion on Day 30, and she did it with a grace that shocked everyone, including herself. I heard she wrote him a three-page letter, then threw it away, then wrote one sentence and gave him that. As far as I can tell, they’re both doing okay. They sat together at lunch last week, arguing about nothing, laughing the way people do when they’ve finally stopped pretending.

On Day 36, she cornered me by the lockers.

“Sword Boy. I am going to say this exactly once.”

“Okay.”

“You are not allowed to make it weird.”

“I never make anything weird.”

She gave me a look that could have peeled paint.

“Okay. I make some things weird.”

She crossed her arms. Her grey eyes went to the ceiling, the floor, the nearest poster about hand-washing, anywhere but me. “The sword on your notebook. In Grade 11. The one I said was not completely terrible. There was a letter K carved in the hilt.”

I felt my whole body go cold and then hot. “You saw that.”

“Obviously I saw that. I notice everything.” The tips of her ears went red. “I let it happen. It was flattering. Disgustingly flattering. I pretended I hadn’t seen, and I was awful to you for seven months so I wouldn’t have to think about it.”

“That’s… actually really bad strategy.”

“I am aware.”

I laughed. She almost laughed. She bit it back and looked furious about it.

“I did like you,” I said. “For a long time. But I think I liked the idea of you more than I knew you. And I’m sorry, if that’s rude.”

“It’s not rude. It’s accurate.” She straightened her sleeve. “I was never going to be your first choice anyway. I’d have criticized your hairline.”

“I’m not going bald.”

“Yet.”

Then she went quiet, and her voice came out smaller. “You’ve been looking at someone else for twenty-six days.”

“Twenty-nine.”

“Spare me the exact count.” She sniffed. “I was irritated at the canteen. Don’t misunderstand. That was not feelings. You used to be mine to insult, and then she appeared, with her ribbon and her handkerchief, looking at you like you were a dish she hadn’t figured out yet. I did not care for it. I still don’t.” A pause. “But she looks at you properly. I noticed that too.”

I didn’t know what to say. I opened my mouth and the truth fell out instead. “Thank you, Kale.”

“Do not thank me. It makes me feel unwell.” She turned to go, then stopped. “Friends. Provisionally. Barely.”

“Barely is fine.”

“It had better be.” And she walked off with her chin high and her ponytail swinging, the most dramatic exit in Grade 12, and I walked in the opposite direction feeling lighter than I’d felt in months.

Day39The Night Before

The farewell party was the next day, and everyone was already getting sentimental. People were exchanging contact numbers, signing each other’s shirts, promising to “totally keep in touch,” which we all know is a lie people tell with love.

Kris and I skipped the planning meeting and sat on the steps of the old building instead.

“The program ends tomorrow,” she said.

“I know.”

“We will return to our own schools. Opposite sides of the city.”

“I know.”

She was sitting perfectly upright, as always, with her hands in her lap. But her thumb was rubbing against the knuckle of her other hand, over and over, a small nervous motion I’d learned meant she was rehearsing something.

“I want it on record,” she said, “that this is not a confession.”

“Okay.”

“It is an observation.”

“Okay.”

“I have noticed you at a frequency that is statistically unreasonable.” She stared straight ahead. “I noticed your sword. I noticed your cat. I noticed that you give people the last fry, even when they’ve stolen the rest. I noticed that when I said something unkind, you did not flinch, you waited, and then you said something kind back, and I did not know what to do with that.”

“Kris…”

“I am not finished.” Her ear was red. Her voice shook, just barely, like a spoon in a teacup. “I do not like many people. I do not say it. I was taught that saying it gives people leverage. But you do not seem like someone who would use it as leverage, so.” She swallowed. “I like you. Is that sufficiently clear?”

My hands were shaking. I made a fist to stop them. “I liked you on Day 3. With the crackers.”

She turned and stared at me. “That was Day 3.”

“I know. I thought it was indigestion.”

She made a sound I had never heard from her before. It was a laugh, but it came out like a hiccup, and she clapped a hand over her mouth in horror. Then she laughed again, harder, and this time she didn’t stop it. She laughed so loudly that a group of juniors turned to stare, and I laughed too, and we sat on the steps like two idiots who had finally said the thing.

Then, slowly, carefully, as if I were approaching a startled deer, I closed the two inches.

I took her hand.

That’s it. That’s all it was. No music, no kiss, no speech. Just her fingers, cool and a little trembling, finding their way through mine. It turns out real love is not very cinematic.

“Is this okay?” I said.

She was quiet for a very long time. Then she squeezed my hand, once.

“This is acceptable,” she said.

“Acceptable is good.”

“Acceptable,” she said, smiling at the dark, “is acceptable.”

Day40The Letters

The closing ceremony was long and loud and full of speeches I won’t remember. Ma’am Reyes cried. Mico gave a toast that was mostly about snacks. Dion shook my hand and said, “Good luck, bro,” and he meant it. Kale stood at the back with her arms crossed and mouthed one word at me across the gym. I’m fairly sure it was “Weird.” I’m also fairly sure it was affectionate.

At the very end, they handed back our letters.

I opened mine on the bleachers. Dear Case, I hope you were brave. At least a little.

I was. I think I really was.

Kris sat beside me and opened hers. She read it silently, and her face did something complicated. Then she folded it along the original creases, with her usual precision, and handed it to me without a word.

In her perfect handwriting, in blue ink, it said:

Dear Kris, there is a boy in Group 6 who draws swords that are always losing and burns garlic with total confidence. Do not fall for him. You will anyway. When you do, allow yourself to be ordinary about it. Ordinary is not a failure.

I looked up. Her eyes were bright and shiny and trying very hard not to be.

“I wrote that on Day 21,” she said stiffly. “It was a prediction. My predictions are generally accurate.”

“On Day 21? You wrote you will anyway on Day 21?”

“Do not make it a thing.”

“I’m making it a thing.”

“I know you are.” She pressed her lips together, but they wouldn’t stay flat. “We start properly,” she said. “Not like a drama. Like us. Slowly.”

“Slowly,” I agreed.

“Messages every day. Weekend visits. You will come to Aurelian’s foundation day, and you will wear something presentable.”

“That’s a lot of demands.”

“I have high standards.”

“I noticed.”

She leaned her head on my shoulder, very stiffly at first, like a person trying out a new chair. Then she relaxed. The two white buses were already idling at the gate, engines grumbling, the maroon uniforms slowly gathering to leave. We had maybe five minutes.

We used them.

The End of the Report

Ma’am Reyes, I’m sorry this is so long. You said to write about my life, and I had no idea my life would turn into this when I started.

Here’s what I learned, since every report needs a conclusion.

I used to think love stories were something that happened to other people. The ones with better hair and better timing and better lighting. I thought I was unlovable, not in a dramatic way, just in a quiet, background-character kind of way.

But it turns out love isn’t a scene. It isn’t rain or umbrellas or train stations. It’s a girl who tilts her umbrella toward you and pretends it was an accident. It’s a rival who admits she saw the K in the sword. It’s forty days of showing up, a little at a time, until one night you realize you haven’t been lonely in weeks.

I’m still NGSB, technically. I still get insulted by a platinum-haired girl who calls my lunch a cry for help. I still draw swords that lose.

But I have a number saved in my phone under a name that isn’t a girl’s name, even if it sounds a lot like one. And every night at nine, it buzzes with a message that says, Did you eat? Do not lie. Send a photograph. I will grade it.

Last night, I sent her a picture of garlic rice. A very sad, very burnt garlic rice.

She replied: Acceptable.

And under it, a second message, a few seconds later, as if she couldn’t help herself: More than acceptable.

I think that counts.

I think I’m allowed to say it now.

Unlovable?

Not anymore.
